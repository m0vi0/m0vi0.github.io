Catching deauth floods: a homemade WiFi IDS feeding Wazuh

Kismet on a single-board computer, alerts forwarded over syslog into the SIEM I already run.

  • Kismet
  • Wazuh
  • Syslog
  • WIDS

Most home networks have no visibility into local wireless activity. Deauthentication attacks, which force devices to disconnect so their reassociation can be captured, usually go unnoticed by consumer routers. I already ran Wazuh as my homelab SIEM, so the goal was practical: forward meaningful WiFi IDS alerts into that pipeline, so detection becomes part of a workflow instead of a dashboard nobody checks.

The setup

I repurposed a small single-board computer on the network as a monitoring node running Kismet, with a dedicated WiFi adapter in monitor mode. Its only job is detection: it listens for management frames and raises alerts when they cross configured thresholds. A low-cost device keeps the footprint small and easy to replace.

Kismet alerts and DEAUTHFLOOD

Kismet has an alert type for high rates of deauthentication frames (DEAUTHFLOOD). It's rate-based per source, so normal roaming and transient noise don't trigger it, and the threshold is configurable to match a given environment.

Forwarding alerts into Wazuh

Kismet exposes recent alerts through its web endpoint. I wrote a lightweight poller that watches that feed, deduplicates by alert timestamp, and writes each new alert to the local syslog. The Wazuh agent collects syslog, so the SIEM ingests and correlates wireless events alongside everything else.

Wazuh decoder and rule

To make the alerts meaningful in Wazuh, I added a small custom decoder and rule to the local ruleset. The decoder matches the forwarded syslog tag and extracts the alert type and text; the rule fires on DEAUTHFLOOD and sets a severity and description. Wazuh then treats these like any other security telemetry, which opens up notifications or ticketing downstream.

Tuning

Wireless environments are noisy, and what looks like an attack in one home can be normal in another. I checked the DEAUTHFLOOD threshold against day-to-day baseline traffic and adjusted it to cut false positives. Deduplication in the poller and sensible rule levels in Wazuh keep alert storms down. For anything beyond a homelab, the poller should run as a managed service with credentials in environment variables or a secrets store, not hard-coded.

Notifications

Alerts go out through my existing mail relay, so a significant event produces an email instead of sitting in an unattended dashboard. I kept the mail path simple on purpose; webhooks, an alert manager or ticketing would be the next step.

What I'd improve

Polling is simple and reliable, but an event-driven approach (a persistent websocket subscription, where supported) would cut detection-to-notification latency and load. Longer term, correlating wireless alerts with other signals in the SIEM, like DHCP logs, client posture or network flows, would put a deauth flood in context and reduce false positives further.
